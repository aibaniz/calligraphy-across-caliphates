Naskh

Historical Context

Naskh (literally “copying”) developed into the standard script for transcribing everyday texts and smaller Qur’anic manuscripts. Although identifying the exact form of Naskh used by early masters like Ibn al-Bawwab is complex, it became widely accepted as one of the most legible and balanced of the “Six Pens” systematized in later centuries. Naskh was commonly paired with larger display scripts such as Thuluth, and over time became the basis for modern Arabic typefaces. Early forms of the script varied regionally and by calligrapher, reflecting a lack of standardized training and style guides in the medieval period.

Visual Characteristics

  • Balanced combination of flat and round forms, with clear, readable letter shapes
  • Equal attention to thick and thin strokes, contributing to harmony on the page
  • Frequently used for continuous text, especially literary works and religious manuscripts
  • Evolved alongside companion scripts like Rayḥān and Tawqīʿ, sometimes blended in early manuscripts
  • Modern Arabic print fonts are derived from later forms of Naskh due to its clarity and legibility
During the reign of the Mughal emperor Awrangzib (r. 1658–1707), the calligrapher ʿAbd al-Bāqī Ḥaddād emigrated to India from Iran, where “in recent times he had borne off the polo ball of precedence among writers of naskh and made naskh the bride of calligraphy by embellishing and adorning it in a new manner.” — Haftqalami, Tadhkirah-i khwashnivisān, p. 125, cited in Bayani, Contadini, and Stanley, The Decorated Word, p. 173 n. 19; quoted in Sheila S. Blair, Islamic Calligraphy, p. 550
